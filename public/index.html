How strong airliners are
Normal flight1 g
Certified with zero damage−1 to +2.5 g
Must not break before−1.5 to +3.75 g
Every airliner is certified to these limits (EASA CS-25 / FAA Part 25). Even severe turbulence rarely goes past ±1 g; the injuries it causes are almost always to people not wearing a seatbelt.
How this works
  • Position comes from the plane's own ADS-B broadcast (adsb.lol, adsb.fi, OpenSky). Over oceans receivers lose it, so the marker moves along the projected path from the last fix, and you can tap the map to correct it.
  • The projected path follows the great circle to your destination from your current heading. The corridor is how far real flights stray from that line (wind-optimised tracks, airways), narrowing as you approach landing.
  • The official ICAO forecasts airlines plan with, from the World Area Forecast System: gridded turbulence and storm tops, and the significant-weather chart. They carry the largest single weight.
  • A multi-model ensemble on the full NOAA GFS, ECMWF IFS and ECMWF AI (AIFS) grids: eight turbulence measures on two cruise layers, mapped onto the official scale the way NCAR's Graphical Turbulence Guidance does it, weighted for your flight level. Their spread sets how wide the g range is.
  • Storms: model instability and rain, forecast storm tops, and live satellite storm detection along the next three hours (Meteosat storm cells and lightning; cold cloud tops from GOES and Himawari).
  • Official warnings (SIGMETs) and recent pilot reports near your path raise the forecast where they apply.
  • Turbulence becomes g through a gust-response model for your aircraft's size and weight (the jolts get bigger as fuel burns off), then a simulation adds patchiness and forecast error.
  • Your reports feed a Gaussian process in time: one term for "this plane feels rougher or smoother than the model", another for "the model is wrong about this patch of weather" that fades over about 40 minutes.
  • How-often figures come from airline turbulence measurements (moderate or worse in about 1% of reports, severe under 0.1%). They're order-of-magnitude numbers.
Open source. Not an operational tool: the crew sees storms on radar, gets real-time reports and routes around the worst. Keep your seatbelt fastened whenever you're seated.
